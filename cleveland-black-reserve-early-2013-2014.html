The short version

Poor early-era bourbon; price does not rescue the imbalance.

Early Cleveland Black Reserve provides a more defensible low-end example than assigning an extreme score from a single critic. Three independent early-era accounts repeatedly describe chemical associations, raw or bitter wood, disproportionate heat and weak integration. The 2.2 assessment belongs to those documented 2013–14 bottles, including Batch 004, not every later Cleveland release. 321

Later Batch 008 receives a materially better assessment. That improvement is retained as a boundary on the judgment, rather than ignored to make a cleaner brand-wide condemnation. The label name alone is insufficient provenance. 4

Background

The name, the era and the limits of the story

Cleveland Whiskey’s early business proposition centered on accelerated wood treatment. The original release attracted attention because a rapid process seemed to offer a very different route from long conventional barrel residence. The historical reviews are useful precisely because they test the sensory result rather than accept the novelty as proof of maturity. 3

By 2015, The Whiskey Room describes a more successful Batch 008, albeit with oak still prominent. That later evidence does not retroactively improve an early sample, but it makes it inappropriate to apply the early 2.2 score to current Black Reserve or an unidentified later batch. 4

What is actually in the bottle?

Specifications without invented precision

The reviewed strength is 100 proof. Mark Hebert reports roughly six months plus a brief treatment period for his early bottle; other early process descriptions vary. No universal maturation figure or original grain percentages are adopted. The current producer recipe would be a different-time specification, not evidence for the early batch. 1

Sipology specifically identifies Batch 004. The other early accounts do not supply matching codes, so the dossier is an explicitly bounded historical-era synthesis rather than a claim that all three tasted the same batch. Confidence is medium, despite strong agreement about the types of defects. 2

Critical synthesis

The flavor evidence, with disagreement intact

The flavor profile synthesizes the credited written reviews. It is not an unperformed first-person tasting. Historical and single-barrel scope stays explicit.

Nose

Solvent or marker-like associations with cedar, green wood and occasional fruit; little stable aromatic integration.

Palate

Sweetness and spice compete with harsh wood/chemical impressions. Texture is too weak to carry the heat cleanly.

Finish

Persistent bitter or chemical/raw-wood aftertaste is the repeated complaint, rather than a merely short finish.

Ginny Tonic and Bob “Pops” White · Bourbon & Banter · November 11, 2013

Ginny Tonic describes chemical/wood imbalance and a harsh lasting aftertaste. Bob White independently reacts very negatively to the sample within the same publication. Their two palates are not counted as two separate source publications. 3

Josh · Sipology · February 7, 2014

Josh’s Batch 004 comparison finds marker-like flavor, heat and poor balance. The exact code is retained; the review’s personal health anecdote is not converted into a claim about toxicity. 2

Mark Hebert · Bear Marks · June 13, 2014

Mark Hebert’s repeated early-era tastings find thinness, bitter raw wood and sap/soap-like associations rather than improving integration. The extended tasting context strengthens the defect assessment. 1

The Whiskey Room · April 2015

The Whiskey Room finds Batch 008 substantially more successful. This later counterpoint is a release boundary and improvement signal, not another vote for the early-era score. 4

Where the judgments differ

The early sources agree more about the defects than their precise tasting vocabulary. All describe disruptive harshness or wood/chemical imbalance. Later improvement shows why the scope must stop at the documented early era. A 2.2 score is poor; it does not require declaring every early sample literally undrinkable or forcing a 1.

Practical use

Put the bottle in the right glass

Evidence and inference: The water observation comes directly from Bourbon & Banter. Other guidance is editorial inference; no serve is claimed to cure the documented defects.

Neat / water

For research, taste a small sample. Bourbon & Banter’s splash of water opens some notes but does not establish a complete repair.

On ice

Cooling may reduce apparent heat, but no evidence shows that it resolves raw wood or chemical imbalance.

Old Fashioned

Sugar and bitters might conceal some defects, but concealment is not demonstrated cocktail quality.

Manhattan

Not recommended from the documented evidence; harsh wood can remain intrusive after mixing.

Highball / cola

A strong mixer could mask flavor, but the source record does not establish a worthwhile rescue serve.

Food / occasion

Most useful for a historical process-comparison tasting with batch provenance recorded, rather than ordinary enjoyment.

Final assessment

Two scores, two different questions

Bourbon Quality
1.0 / 10

The practical bourbon worst anchor is limited to the early 2013–June 2014 evidence, including Sipology’s Batch 004. Bourbon & Banter reports persistent chemical burn and wood pulp; Sipology finds profound solvent-like failure; Bear Marks adds raw wood, bitter soap and a thin body. These independent reports support the floor relative to ordinary bourbon and Michter’s 2024 ceiling. Later improved Batch 008 evidence is excluded from this score.

Value · Original MSRP
1.2 / 10

At the contemporaneous $34.99 reference, defects overwhelm the drinking proposition. Cheaper competent bourbon makes this exceptionally weak value; neither rapid-processing novelty nor historical interest improves the liquid’s value.

Quality is category-relative and excludes price or personal-palate weighting. Value uses the identified $34.99 750 mL reference. The scores are editorial synthesis, not averages of incompatible published rating systems.

Quality scale: 1 catastrophic · 2 extremely poor · 3 poor · 4 below average · 5 average · 6 good · 7 very good · 8 excellent · 9 elite · 10 reference-class. Quality scores are category-relative. A 9.0 Bourbon means elite within Bourbon; a 9.0 Rye means elite within Rye. The numbers are not claims that unlike whiskey styles taste equivalent.
