The short version

The budget bourbon whose restraint makes it more useful

Old Grand-Dad Bottled-in-Bond earns its reputation through a clear combination: high-rye bourbon flavor, a useful 100-proof bottling, a bonded minimum age and a $24.95 NC price. Cinnamon, caramel, citrus and oak recur in the reviews, often with nutty, cocoa or tobacco accents. The result is a direct, characterful bourbon that can cover both neat drinking and the house cocktail role. 1 2 3

The bottle should not be oversold as a remarkably deep bourbon. Its strengths are identity, balance and usefulness, with enough flavor to make the price unusually attractive. Compared with OGD 114, the reduction in proof can make the same family’s spice easier to follow. The value score is therefore much higher than the category quality score, and that is an intentional distinction.

Background

Basil Hayden’s grandson gave the brand its name

The producer dates the first Old Grand-Dad bottling to 1882 and explains the name as a tribute to Basil Hayden Sr. by his grandson. The brand survived changing ownership and the Prohibition period before becoming part of Beam in the late 1980s. That pedigree explains the old-fashioned identity; today’s whiskey is nevertheless a modern Beam-produced bourbon. 4 1

The ordinary bonded bottle is one of the long-standing core expressions, alongside the lower-proof bourbon and OGD 114. Recent seven-year bonded and single-barrel additions have made exact identification more important. This dossier covers the regular NAS 100-proof bottle at the budget NC price, not the separate seven-year release. The producer’s current range shows both. 5

What is actually in the bottle?

A real bond designation, with a reported high-rye recipe

The widely reported mash is 63% corn, 27% rye and 10% malted barley. Breaking Bourbon documents those proportions, while the current producer page describes the high-rye character without printing the numerical split. The percentages are strongly corroborated industry information. Neither rye percentage nor proof alone explains the nutty impression associated with Beam whiskey. 1 5

Bottled-in-Bond establishes 100 proof, at least four years of aging, and production by one distiller at one distillery in a single distilling season. Those are concrete standards, not a promise that every bonded whiskey will taste equally mature or rich. The regular OGD bottle does not carry a separate numerical age statement beyond that designation. 5 6

The whiskey matures in new charred oak as straight bourbon, with no disclosed secondary finish. It is bottled at a fixed 50% ABV and is not cask strength. Public information consulted does not support assigning this exact release a numerical char level, a precise warehouse position or a filtration method merely because other Beam expressions use one.

Critical synthesis

A flavor profile, with the disagreements intact

These notes synthesize the named reviews below; they are not a first-person tasting of a newly opened bottle. Older reviews supply perspective on the standard expression, while current specifications come from current product sources.

Nose

Caramel, brown sugar, vanilla, cinnamon, citrus and toasted wood. Nut or cocoa impressions appear in favorable accounts.

Palate

Rye-derived spice and sweetness coexist with orange peel, char and nutty grain. The 100-proof frame is assertive without requiring the intensity of 114.

Finish

Pepper, cinnamon and dry oak recur, sometimes with leather or tobacco. Length varies, but the close generally supplies enough flavor for its everyday role.

Breaking Bourbon · 2020

The review centers on cinnamon, caramel, vanilla, fruit and char, with a medium-bodied delivery and a sweet-spicy finish. Its praise concerns a recognizable bourbon that remains useful across sipping and mixing. 1

Michael Veach · 2017

The bourbon historian’s tasting finds citrus, berries, vanilla, leather, tobacco and oak, followed by a long peppery close. That richer account is a check against reducing the whiskey to heat and peanut notes alone. 2

Wine Enthusiast · Kara Newman, 2015

The blind-tasting account highlights brown sugar, cocoa, hazelnut and toffee, with good persistence and resilience to dilution. It provides a favorable structural perspective alongside the more spice-led description from Breaking Bourbon. 3

Where the judgments differ

Some accounts emphasize cinnamon and straightforward medium weight; others find more citrus, cocoa and persistence. The disagreement is about how much richness accompanies the common spicy structure. Nothing in the dossier requires a drinker to like Beam’s nutty or dry accents. The value case is especially strong for people who already appreciate them.

Price & value

What the current price buys

NC ABC regular retail · 750 mL · August–October 2026 7

Reference checked October 4, 2026. Listing is not a local-store inventory guarantee; temporary monthly sale prices are excluded.

At $24.95 in NC, the regular bonded bottle is only $3 above Evan Williams Bonded and $3 below Wild Turkey 101 Bourbon. That is unusually favorable territory for a distinctive, 100-proof bourbon with a documented bonded minimum. It remains worth assessing on its own quality, but there is little reason to pay substantially more for a basic cocktail base if this flavor profile suits the drink.

Best ways to enjoy

Put the bottle in the right glass

Evidence and inference: Veach discusses Manhattan use, Newman reports resilience to water, and the producer recommends classic cocktails. The specific adjustments remain editorial guidance. 2 3 5

Neat / a few drops of water

Start neat to determine whether the cinnamon, citrus and nutty core appeal. A modest splash of water is reasonable if the spice crowds out the fruit. Its bonded proof gives more dilution room than an 80- or 90-proof mixer, but that does not mean unlimited water will preserve its structure.

One large cube

The bond proof supports measured dilution. Slow cooling can ease cinnamon and expose citrus; a large cube avoids rapidly washing out the medium body.

Old Fashioned

Producer guidance supports classic bourbon cocktails. Editorially, its cinnamon and citrus should remain identifiable with modest syrup and bitters, making it a strong low-cost house option.

Manhattan

Veach specifically identifies it as useful here. The spice, citrus and oak give the vermouth something to meet; restrained sweetness protects the drier end of the bourbon.

The bourbon makes an especially practical Old Fashioned or Manhattan base: flavorful enough to remain identifiable, inexpensive enough to replenish without ceremony. It also works as a reference bottle when comparing high-rye bourbon against wheated or lower-rye styles. Buy the 114 only when the added intensity is a feature you actually want.

Where it is less useful

Less ideal: extremely sweet builds that conceal the high-rye spice, or buying it at premium-bourbon prices merely for the bond designation.
