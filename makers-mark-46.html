The short version

The sensible next step beyond the red-wax original

Maker’s Mark 46 is a convincing example of an everyday upgrade with an identifiable result. The familiar wheated base gains more roasted oak, cinnamon, clove and darker sweetness, with fruit still present beneath the wood. Reviewers generally find a more developed and better-defined bourbon than the classic 90-proof Maker’s, without the intensity of a cask-strength bottle. 1 2

The extra wood does not simply make the whiskey sweeter. Dry oak, leather and spice recur in the ending, and the texture remains moderate in several accounts. Its strongest case is a useful expansion of the Maker’s profile at a small NC premium, not a claim that finishing transforms the base into an exceptionally mature bourbon.

Background

46 names the stave profile, not the years

Maker’s introduced 46 in 2010, developing a secondary wood treatment with Independent Stave Company. The number identifies the selected stave profile. It is not an age statement, and the regular bottling is not 46% ABV. The expression created a foundation for the producer’s later wood-finishing experiments and Private Selection program. 3 4

Packaging has changed. Breaking Bourbon’s 2022 review discusses the move away from the older tall presentation toward the brand’s more familiar bottle shape. The current producer imagery shows another recognizable 46 presentation. Those changes are useful for identifying bottles, but the dossier relies on the current 94-proof specification rather than treating a package revision as evidence of a new age or recipe. 1 3

What is actually in the bottle?

Ten seared French oak staves reshape the base

The base is Maker’s wheated bourbon. The often-reported grain split is 70% corn, 16% soft red winter wheat and 14% malted barley; the producer confirms the wheat choice but the exact percentages are industry reporting. The added oak changes maturation, not the mash. 5 2

The producer describes placing ten seared French oak staves in barrels of fully matured Maker’s bourbon for further maturation in its limestone cellar. This is a stave treatment in the existing barrel environment, not a previously used wine cask. The process adds another kind of wood influence without importing a prior spirit’s flavor. 3

The regular 46 is bottled at 94 proof, or 47% ABV, and carries no numerical age statement. Even if the bourbon enters its finishing stage at cask strength, the retail expression remains a fixed-proof bottling. Maker’s Mark 46 Cask Strength is a separate product. A cellar or stave description also cannot establish one exact finishing duration for every current bottle. 3

Critical synthesis

A flavor profile, with the disagreements intact

These notes synthesize the named reviews below; they are not a first-person tasting of a newly opened bottle. Older reviews supply perspective on the standard expression, while current specifications come from current product sources.

Nose

Caramel, toasted vanilla, fragrant oak, cinnamon and clove. Fruit can include apricot, citrus or baked apple.

Palate

Darker sweetness meets roasted oak and baking spice. Fruit gives range, though the body usually remains moderate rather than thick.

Finish

Dry wood, cinnamon, clove and citrus linger. Some accounts add leather, tobacco and heat; the finish is not uniformly as rich as the nose.

Breaking Bourbon · Jordan Moskal, 2022

The review finds a rich, sweet, oak-led nose and a balanced palate, but a less impressive finish with dry wood, leather and lingering heat. It treats the expression as a worthwhile upgrade whose familiar price is a major strength. 1

The Whiskey Shelf

This account welcomes caramel, apricot, orange, roasted oak, cinnamon and clove, finding a more finished-feeling bourbon than standard Maker’s. It still describes ordinary viscosity and warns that the pronounced oak spice will not suit everyone. 2

Wine Enthusiast · Kara Newman, 2013

The earlier blind-tasting review emphasizes lively vanilla and fruit with ginger, clove and other warm spices. It supports the broader sweet-and-spicy identity, while remaining a historical bottle assessment rather than a current batch test. 6

Where the judgments differ

The main issue is how much oak the drinker welcomes. The finishing treatment gives the bourbon greater definition, but that definition includes drying spice. Reviewers who dislike the base Maker’s softness can appreciate the added structure; someone expecting only extra caramel may find the oak more forceful than anticipated. Greater flavor distinction and greater viscosity should not be treated as the same improvement.

Price & value

What the current price buys

At $39.95 in NC, 46 costs only $6 more than classic Maker’s Mark. That is a persuasive premium for a clearly different flavor profile and a small proof increase. It also competes at the same price as Knob Creek 9 and standard Woodford, so the value case remains style-specific: French oak finishing rather than an explicit nine-year age or a gentler traditional bourbon.

Best ways to enjoy

Put the bottle in the right glass

Evidence and inference: The producer specifically features a 46 Manhattan. Ice and sweetener adjustments are editorial inferences from the reviews’ moderate body and dry oak-spice ending. 3 1 2

Neat / a few drops of water

Taste 46 neat beside classic Maker’s if possible. The useful question is whether fruit and caramel remain balanced against the added roast and clove, not whether you can guess the stave count from the glass. Use modest water; the regular bottle already sits at a manageable proof.

One large cube

Cooling can soften clove and heat, but it may also mute the fruit that balances the oak. Use one cube and limited melt; extra ice does not remove wood tannin.

Manhattan

The producer supplies a 46 Manhattan recipe. French oak spice and caramel make a plausible partner for vermouth; editorially, restrained sweetness helps preserve the drier finish.

Old Fashioned

Editorial inference: a little syrup and orange peel can connect caramel, fruit and roasted wood. Too much sugar flattens the flavor into toast-and-sweetness rather than adding texture.

Its price and more definite flavor make it suitable for a bourbon Old Fashioned, particularly with restrained syrup. A neat evening pour often shows the finishing treatment more clearly. Drinkers sensitive to dry oak should sample before assuming that a wheated base will make the whiskey uniformly soft.

Where it is less useful

Less ideal: assuming a wheated base guarantees a soft finish, or using strongly sweet builds that conceal the fruit beneath the added oak.

Final assessment

Two scores, two different questions

Bourbon Quality
6.6 / 10

Breaking Bourbon, the Whiskey Shelf and Wine Enthusiast support more developed toasted fruit and spice than standard Maker’s, with only moderate body and finish evolution. It clearly exceeds the ordinary Maker’s/Bulleit midpoint and avoids the low anchor’s failed oak integration. Its distinct finishing places it near the stronger everyday shelf, above Double Oaked on the combined balance evidence, but below Knob Creek 9 and far from Michter’s ceiling.

Value · NC ABC
8.1 / 10

Retain 8.1 at $39.95. The small premium over $33.95 standard Maker’s buys a documented flavor improvement, preserving excellent value for this particular style.
