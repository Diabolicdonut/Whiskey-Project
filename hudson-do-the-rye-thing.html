The short version

Below-average overall evidence; a strong positive counterpoint remains.

Do The Rye Thing is a useful reminder that a numerical score should not hide how reviewers disagree. Breaking Bourbon likes its delicate fruit, while Thirty-One Whiskey finds rich apple character and successful cocktails. Bourbon Culture reports severe bitterness and distracting off notes; a later NYC review also finds unpleasant dusty earth. The spread is too substantial to manufacture a clean consensus. 3425

The resulting 4.3 Quality assessment sits below the ordinary American-rye midpoint, without treating the whiskey as a universally catastrophic release. At a current alternate-retail reference of $39.99, lower-priced established rye gives this a weak general buying argument. A satisfactory sample is the practical reason to depart from that conclusion. 67

Background

The name, the era and the limits of the story

Hudson’s September 2020 rebrand replaced the Manhattan Rye name with Do The Rye Thing and introduced the conspicuous green-label design. The current producer publishes a three-year minimum, 92 proof and a 95/5 rye-and-malted-barley recipe. Those specifications identify this dossier’s subject; earlier bottles with another name or finishing treatment are separate research questions. 31

The history of William Grant & Sons involves a Hudson brand transaction in 2010 and a later Tuthilltown distillery transaction in 2017. Erenzo’s interview is useful because it separates the two. Accounts that flatten both events into a single acquisition date can also make the liquid’s chronology seem simpler than it was. 8

The producer presents the current whiskey as New York straight rye and Empire Rye certified. That local identity is meaningful context, but it awards no automatic sensory points. Likewise, a bright new label is not evidence that the old Maple Cask criticism applies, or that all reviewers must find the new liquid improved. 1

What is actually in the bottle?

Specifications without invented precision

Current producer-confirmed facts are unusually straightforward: 95% rye, 5% malted barley, at least three years and 46% ABV. The bottle is a straight rye with no named secondary-cask finish. The minimum age does not establish that every barrel is exactly three years old. 1

Thirty-One Whiskey describes on-site hybrid-pot distillation and maturation in new charred oak. Those are credited reporting rather than a new, comprehensive producer specification. Exact char number, individual warehouse placement, chill-filtration practice and reviewed-batch codes are not established here. The recipe’s similarity to MGP’s common 95/5 does not show that the whiskey came from MGP. 4

Bourbon Culture offers hypotheses about age and grain quality to explain the disliked profile. Neither hypothesis is a confirmed cause. A documented minimum can help explain a youthful impression, but no sensory review alone proves faulty grain or production. The dossier distinguishes the experience from speculation about why it occurred. 2

Critical synthesis

The flavor evidence, with disagreement intact

The flavor profile synthesizes the credited written reviews. It is not an unperformed first-person tasting. Historical and single-barrel scope stays explicit.

Nose

The favorable side emphasizes honey, light orchard fruit, mint and baking spice. The negative side reports cardboard, fresh wood and dusty earth. These are alternatives found across reviews, not a claim that every bottle combines all of them.

Palate

Bright fruit and mint can make the whiskey approachable, while some reviewers encounter green, marker-like or earthy interruptions. Body ranges from delicate to richer in the published accounts.

Finish

A short grain-and-pepper close in the launch review contrasts with a rich spicy finish in the favorable account and excessive citrus bitterness or sour oak in the negative one. Batch matching is insufficient to resolve the split.

Eric Hasman · Breaking Bourbon · December 23, 2020

Hasman finds a satisfying, delicate fruit-led rye, but considers the grain and low maturity the principal limits. His launch review supplies a positive middle position, rather than an elite-quality claim. 3

Nick Leghorn · Thirty-One Whiskey · April 22, 2022

Leghorn gives 4.5/5 and finds rich apple, cherry and baking spice without offensive bitterness. He also directly discusses ice, an Old Fashioned and a mule. That is substantive positive evidence and must stay in the synthesis. 4

Mike · Bourbon Culture · February 16, 2022

Mike’s 3/10 review finds pleasant mint and fruit interrupted by marker, cardboard and sour oak impressions. His unusually bitter finish drives a severe conclusion, though his proposed production causes remain speculative. 2

Christopher · NYC Whiskey Review · February 14, 2024

Christopher’s shorter 2024 account dislikes dusty wood and earth. It offers later negative corroboration but less detail, so it receives lower weight than the full reviews; its quoted price is not the current price basis. 5

Where the judgments differ

Two favorable accounts and two negative ones do not produce a reliable number by arithmetic. The negative flavor interruptions are more consequential than merely wanting more intensity, while the substantive favorable reviews prevent calling the expression inherently undrinkable. Neither matching batch codes nor a direct comparative tasting establishes a cause. The 4.3 is a cautious category position with medium-low confidence.

Consumer context

A reader comment on Thirty-One Whiskey finds honey, fruit and mint, supplying a small positive consumer counterpoint. It is one unverified bottle, not a batch survey. The useful consumer action is to sample the current green-label expression rather than purchase based on an earlier Hudson name or on one enthusiastic score. 4

Practical use

Put the bottle in the right glass

Evidence and inference: Leghorn supplies direct ice and cocktail observations, and the producer supplies a Ryeball recipe. Manhattan and cautionary adjustments are editorial inferences from the conflicting documented profiles. 41

Neat / sample first

A small neat pour is the best test of whether the bottle resembles the attractive fruit profile or the harsh counteraccounts. No specific serving trick resolves that disagreement.

One large cube

Leghorn reports that ice reduces force without eliminating the fruit and spice in his sample. Treat that as direct positive evidence, not a guarantee for a bottle with bitter or dusty impressions. 4

Old Fashioned

The favorable reviewer finds bitters complementary and recommends very restrained sweetness. Start with less sugar than usual if the sample already tastes sweet; the negative reports still argue against buying solely for this use. 4

Mule / Ryeball

Leghorn directly praises a ginger-beer mule. The producer offers a different Ryeball with sweet vermouth, lime and ginger ale. Both support a ginger-and-citrus direction, without proving every bottle performs equally well. 41

Manhattan

Mint and rye spice could suit vermouth, but no direct comparison here establishes an advantage over the established cheaper cocktail ryes. That suggestion is editorial inference.

Less ideal uses

Avoid heavy dilution before checking the neat sample, or adding large amounts of syrup to solve bitterness. Buy a bottle for its demonstrated character in your glass, rather than expecting packaging or local origin to compensate.
