Old Forester 100 Bourbon

The regular 100-proof Old Forester provides a robust house style at $29.95. Its dry, sometimes bitter oak is the principal reason a drinker may love it or leave it.

The short version

Cherry, chocolate and oak without the Whiskey Row premium

Old Forester 100 Bourbon is one of the most useful introductions to Brown-Forman’s darker bourbon vocabulary. Reviewers repeatedly identify caramel, cherry, chocolate or coffee, nuts, oak and warm spice. The strength gives those flavors practical reach in cocktails, while the price stays well below the producer’s Whiskey Row expressions. 1 2 3

The limitation is not a shortage of recognizable flavor. It is the relationship between those flavors and the whiskey’s body or ending. Breaking Bourbon finds less texture than the aromatic richness implies, followed by a mildly bitter, overly oaky aftertaste. Bourbon Culture welcomes the tannins and spice more readily. This is a character purchase, and the dry finish deserves as much attention as the sweet opening. 1 2

Character for everyday money

Signature 100 proof · cherry, chocolate and robust oak

Background

Signature strength in the core range

Old Forester’s history reaches back to George Garvin Brown and the brand’s 1870 founding. The ordinary 86- and 100-proof bourbons remain the core points of entry into that family. The 100-proof bottle is often called Signature, a name linked to Brown’s signature rather than to a distinct finishing treatment or age statement. 3

The later Whiskey Row releases and barrel-proof offerings changed the brand’s enthusiast reputation, but the standard 100-proof bottle remains a separate everyday expression. It should not be confused with the 1897 Bottled-in-Bond bourbon, a 100-proof single barrel or a store pick. The NC pricing database places it in a bonded-related grouping, but the current producer identity takes precedence: ordinary Old Forester 100 is not labeled Bottled-in-Bond. 4 5

What is actually in the bottle?

A fixed-proof bourbon, without a bond promise

Independent whiskey reporting gives the standard bourbon recipe as 72% corn, 18% rye and 10% malted barley. The current product page confirms the bottling’s identity and strength but does not print those percentages. They are therefore reported recipe information. The same published proportions associated with Woodford do not make the two finished whiskeys identical. 2 3 4

The current bottle is Kentucky straight bourbon at 100 proof, or 50% ABV, matured in new charred oak without a disclosed secondary finish. Its NAS status means there is no numerical age statement to compare directly with Knob Creek 9. A reviewer’s estimate of roughly four years is a perception or reported estimate, not a producer guarantee. 4 2

The 100-proof specification is controlled at bottling, so the product is not cask strength. It carries no bond designation and therefore supplies no promise of a single distiller’s season through that term. Producer-level cooperage and maturation practices can explain context, but they do not establish a precise char level or warehouse position for this individual batch.

Critical synthesis

A flavor profile, with the disagreements intact

These notes synthesize the named reviews below; they are not a first-person tasting of a newly opened bottle. Older reviews supply perspective on the standard expression, while current specifications come from current product sources.

Nose

Caramel, cherry, chocolate, oak and occasional coffee-like earthiness. The aroma often feels richer than the price suggests.

Palate

Maple or caramel sweetness meets nuts, chocolate, oak and baking spice. Accounts differ on whether the mouthfeel is adequately creamy or unexpectedly thin.

Finish

Pepper, clove, tobacco and dry oak. Some reviewers enjoy the tannic structure; another identifies a distracting bitter, over-oaked aftertaste.

Breaking Bourbon · 2019

The review finds caramel and cherry aromatics, followed by oak, pecan and maple-like sweetness. It praises substantial flavor but questions the thinner mouthfeel and a bitter, slightly musty oak impression in the close. 1

Bourbon Culture · 2021

This more favorable account emphasizes chocolate, caramel, cherry, baking spice, tobacco and drying tannins. It appreciates the character available at a modest price and treats the oak structure as a feature more than a defect. 2

The Whiskey Reviewer · Richard Thomas, 2019

Thomas describes brown sugar, caramel, cherry, earthy coffee and a creamy delivery, then a pepper-and-toast finish. The assessment reinforces traditional bourbon balance while adding another view of the texture. 3

Where the judgments differ

The recurring oak bitterness divides the critics. A palate drawn to cherry, cocoa and dry tobacco can find the finish coherent with the whole sip. A palate seeking sweeter fruit and a clean close may find it a blemish. That difference should guide the buying decision more than a blanket claim that 100 proof must be better than a lower-proof bourbon.

Price & value

What the current price buys

At $29.95, the standard bottle costs $5 more than OGD Bonded and $2 more than Wild Turkey 101. That is a modest premium for a distinctive cherry-and-chocolate direction, and a large saving against the producer’s premium range. Its value is excellent if that dry Brown-Forman finish appeals, though it is not the least expensive route to 100-proof cocktail strength.

Best ways to enjoy

Put the bottle in the right glass

Evidence and inference: These recommendations are editorial inferences from confirmed proof and the reviewers’ cherry, cocoa, tannin and texture evidence; they are not a bartender-consensus claim. 1 2 3

Neat / a few drops of water

Try a neat pour and concentrate on the close. The aromatic cherry and caramel are attractive in several reviews, but whether the oak bitterness remains pleasant will determine how often you reach for the bottle. A small splash of water may help distinguish pepper and tobacco from alcohol heat.

One large cube

Cooling may soften the pepper, though wood bitterness can remain as sweetness is muted. One cube preserves the 100-proof frame while allowing a gradual test of the dry finish.

Bourbon Manhattan

Editorial inference: cherry, cocoa and tobacco can align with sweet vermouth. Keep sweetness and dilution balanced so the dry oak supports the drink rather than becoming a bitter aftertaste.

Old Fashioned

Editorial inference: a little sugar can cushion the dry finish, and orange peel supports the fruit. Heavy bitters may stack more bitterness on the tannic oak identified in the critical review.

A bourbon Manhattan can make good use of the darker fruit and cocoa notes, while an Old Fashioned gives the oak a sweeter counterweight. The price and proof make regular mixing sensible. Drinkers who dislike dry, bitter barrel flavors should compare a sample with Wild Turkey or Buffalo Trace before making it their default bourbon.

Where it is less useful

Less ideal: very bitter builds or long dilution when the dry oak is already the part of the bourbon you like least.
